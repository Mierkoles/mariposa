# Naufragios: the story bible

Season one of Mariposa. An adaptation of the *Relación* of Álvar Núñez Cabeza de Vaca (Zamora, 1542), the account of the Narváez expedition to Florida and the eight years it took four survivors to walk out.

Read this before writing any chapter. The running summary in `CONTENT-GUIDE.md` says where the last chapter left off; this file says where the whole thing is going.

## Logline

Florida, 1528. Three hundred Spaniards land looking for gold. Eight years later four men walk into Sinaloa naked, and the Spanish slavers who find them do not believe they are Spanish. One of them wrote it down.

## Why this story

- It is a frontier survival narrative in the original Spanish. The top of the ramp lands on the real 1542 text, lightly modernized.
- The philosophy is carried, not stated. Cabeza de Vaca does not explain the healings or his survival. He reports and moves on. Keep that.
- Mystery and magic stay intact. Mala Cosa, the healings, the weeping on the beach. Never resolve them. He did not.
- Every date and river checks. That makes the no-fabrication rule enforceable.
- The vocabulary it teaches is the core a person actually needs: body, food, weather, water, walking, hunger, cold, family, giving and taking.

## Voice

First person, present tense, Cabeza de Vaca narrating as it happens. He is a royal treasurer, a counter of things, and it shows: he notices quantities, distances, what was given and what was taken. Plain, physical, unhurried. He records wonders in the same register as rations.

Present tense because that is where the first verbs live (level 5, short present-tense clauses). The preterite arrives at level 8, which is also when the four begin the long walk and the narrator's own tense catches up with the source.

Rules from the fiction voice apply: show, never preach; exposition through fragments; no narrator intrusion; no modern language or anachronism; violence fast and costly; flag inventions as inventions.

## Cast

Names as the source gives them. Invent nothing about a real person that the record contradicts. Interior life, dialogue, and day-to-day texture are ours; facts are theirs.

**Álvar Núñez Cabeza de Vaca** (b. Jerez de la Frontera, c. 1490). Treasurer and provost marshal of the expedition. His name means "head of a cow"; family legend says an ancestor, a shepherd, marked a mountain pass with a cow's skull before the battle of Las Navas de Tolosa (1212) and the king gave him the name. Careful, literal, stubborn about the record. Argues against Narváez on the beach and asks the notary to write down that he did. Becomes, in order: castaway, servant, trader, healer, walker, witness. Never explains what he cannot explain.

**Pánfilo de Narváez.** Governor. One eye; lost the other in Mexico fighting Cortés in 1520. Wants gold and a province. Not a fool, but a man who cannot hear a no. Leaves the ships. Leaves his men at sea with the line that ends his command: it is no longer time for one man to command another; each should do what he thinks best to save his own life.

**Andrés Dorantes de Carranza.** Captain. Owner of Estebanico. Survives the wreck on the same island. Steady, practical.

**Alonso del Castillo Maldonado.** Captain. Son of a physician from Salamanca. The first of the four the Indians press into healing, and the most reluctant.

**Estebanico** (Esteban de Dorantes). Born in Azemmour, Morocco. Enslaved. The fourth survivor and, once the four are walking, their forward scout and their voice: he goes ahead, learns the signs, talks to everyone. The source credits him in half-sentences. Give him a full interior and let the reader see how much of the crossing was his. Do not invent a biography for him beyond Azemmour and enslavement; give him thoughts, choices, and judgment instead. His later death at Hawikuh in 1539 is the epilogue's hardest fact.

**Lope de Oviedo.** The strongest man on the barge, the one who climbs the tree on the island. Stays. Turns back every year when Cabeza de Vaca comes for him. Finally walks south with him in the fourth year and then turns back for good with the Deaguanes women. Never seen again.

**Alonso de Solís.** Royal inspector. Drowns when the barge is relaunched at Malhado.

**Fray Juan Suárez.** Commissary, the senior friar. Sides with Narváez on the beach. Dies at sea.

**Hernando de Esquivel.** The last man alive at the Xamho camp on the coast, having eaten the others. Later killed by the Mariames over a dream.

**Figueroa.** One of the four swimmers sent along the coast toward Pánuco. Carries the story of how the other barges ended.

**Diego de Alcaraz.** Captain of the slaving party the four meet on the Río Sinaloa in 1536. Wants the Indians who walked them in.

**Melchior Díaz.** Alcalde mayor at Culiacán. Receives the four well, weeps, and asks their help to call the people back down from the mountains.

**The peoples.** Named as the source names them, with a note where a modern name is known: Apalachee; the Capoques and Han of Malhado (the Karankawa); Charrucos; Deaguanes; Quevenes; Mariames; Yguases; Avavares; Maliacones; Arbadaos; the "people of the cows" at La Junta de los Ríos; the people of Corazones in Sonora. They are people with their own reasons, never scenery, and never a chorus. Their generosity, cruelty, humor, and grief are all in the source. Use all of it.

## Structure

Three arcs of about thirty chapters. Ninety is a target, not a quota. Merge or split beats as pacing demands, but the arc hinges are fixed: the barges scatter, the four reunite at the prickly pear grounds, the horseshoe nail.

The arcs are story structure only. The weave does not step at arc boundaries: the share of Spanish climbs about one point per chapter from the first chapter to the last, and the grammar ladder adds one thing every nine chapters or so. The ramp is defined in `CONTENT-GUIDE.md`. The last column below says which domain the Spanish comes from in each arc, not how much of it there is.

| Arc | Chapters | Dates | Spanish arrives as |
|---|---|---|---|
| I. La expedición | 1 to 30 | Apr 1528 to Nov 1528 | Cognate nouns of ships, ranks, weather, sea. Articles attach, then adjectives |
| II. Malhado | 31 to 60 | Nov 1528 to late 1534 | Body, food, cold, family. Fixed phrases, then present-tense verbs: camino, tengo hambre, duermo. Whole sentences by the end |
| III. Hijos del sol | 61 to 90 | 1535 to 1537, epilogue to 1559 | Preterite. Paragraphs in Spanish. Passages of the modernized 1542 text |

Level markers below ("Level N begins") show where each rung of the ladder starts, so a beat can be read against what the Spanish is allowed to do that day.

### Arc I. La expedición (chapters 1 to 30)

Everything here is from the *Relación* unless marked.

1. **The beach, April 1528.** Name and what it means. Treasurer. Narváez and his one eye. Three hundred men, forty-two horses of the eighty that sailed. An empty village. A gold rattle in the fishing nets.
2. **Memory: Sanlúcar, 17 June 1527.** Five ships, six hundred people. The commission: govern from the Río de las Palmas to the cape of Florida. Hispaniola, where a hundred and forty desert.
3. **Memory: the hurricane at Trinidad, Cuba.** Sixty men and twenty horses gone, two ships lost. Cabeza de Vaca ashore hears the storm carry voices, bells, flutes, tambourines. Winter in Cuba.
4. **The council on the beach.** Narváez wants to march inland and send the ships along the coast to find a harbor. Cabeza de Vaca objects: no food, no interpreter, no knowledge of the land. Narváez: if the treasurer is afraid he may take charge of the ships. He refuses. Asks the notary for a written record.
5. **Taking possession.** The notary, the flags, the King's requirement read to people who are not there. Indians appear and by signs tell them to leave.
6. **The march north, 1 May.** Two pounds of biscuit and half a pound of bacon per man. Fifteen days without a village. Palmettos, sand, pines.
7. **The first river.** Crossing by swimming and on rafts. Indians appear. Guides taken. Corn fields at last.
8. **Dulchanchellin.** A chief carried on another man's back, wearing a painted deer hide, with reed flutes playing. Promises them Apalachee, his enemies.
9. **The Suwannee.** Juan Velázquez rides into the river on horseback and drowns with the horse. The first death of the march. They eat the horse.
10. **Apalachee, late June.** Forty small houses. Corn. Women and children only. The men return with bows. The Spaniards take the chief hostage. Level 2 begins: any concrete noun, articles attach.
11. **The Apalachee war.** Houses burned around them. Arrows through oak as thick as a man's leg. Twenty-five days. There is corn and there is no gold.
12. **Aute.** Nine days south. Ambush at the lagoons. Aute found burned. Corn, beans, squash. Men begin to fall sick.
13. **Looking for the sea.** Cabeza de Vaca sent with the friar and horsemen. Oysters. A shallow bay with no harbor, no ships, nothing.
14. **The conspiracy.** A third of the men sick. The horsemen plan to ride away and leave the sick. Cabeza de Vaca and others shame them out of it.
15. **The decision to build.** No one knows how. One carpenter, a Portuguese. Bellows from deer skin. Nails from stirrups, spurs, and crossbows. Pitch from pine. Rope from palmetto fiber and horse tails.
16. **The Bay of Horses.** A horse killed every third day for meat. The hides become water bags. Forty men die at Aute of sickness and hunger.
17. **Launch, 22 September 1528.** Five barges about ten meters long, forty-seven to forty-nine men each, the gunwale a hand above the water. Sails from shirts. Oars from juniper.
18. **The shallows.** Seven days in water to the waist. An island; canoes; Indians with water jars. A night attack. Narváez struck in the face with a stone. The horsehide water bags rot. Five men die of drinking seawater.
19. **The great river.** Fresh water far out at sea: the Mississippi. The current carries them off before they can land. Level 3 begins: adjectives after nouns.
20. **The barges scatter.** Narváez's barge has the strongest men. Cabeza de Vaca asks for a rope. The answer: it is no time for one man to command another; each should do what seems best to save his life. The governor's barge pulls away. Arc hinge.
21. **Alone.** With the barge of Peñalosa and Téllez for a while, then not. Cold. The men fall over one another; only Cabeza de Vaca and the helmsman can still stand.
22. **6 November 1528.** Dawn, surf, a wave throws the barge onto sand. Lope de Oviedo climbs a tree: an island. He comes back with a pot, a dog, dried mullet, and a hundred archers behind him.
23. **The archers.** Forty Spaniards who cannot stand. Beads and bells offered. The Indians give arrows in pledge and bring fish and roots twice a day.
24. **Relaunch.** They dig the barge out, strip naked, and a wave swamps it. Three drown, Solís among them. Everything is lost. North wind. He writes that they looked like the image of death.
25. **The weeping.** The Indians return, see them naked and broken, sit down among them, and weep loudly for half an hour. Cabeza de Vaca asks to be taken to their houses. Some Spaniards, remembering Mexico, expect to be sacrificed.
26. **The fires.** Carried between fires built along the path so they will not die of cold. The village. A dance all night. Morning: fish and roots. They are alive.
27. **Malhado.** Another barge wrecked nearby: Dorantes, Castillo, their men, Estebanico. Reunion. They name the island Malhado, misfortune.
28. **The swimmers.** Four strong men sent along the coast toward Pánuco, which they believe is near. It is not. Figueroa among them. Level 4 begins: fixed phrases, place phrases.
29. **Winter.** Cold and hunger. At the Xamho camp on the coast five Spaniards eat one another until only Esquivel is left. The Indians are appalled. The Spaniards fear for their lives.
30. **Fifteen.** Of eighty who reached the island, fifteen are alive. A stomach sickness kills half the Indians and they blame the Spaniards. One of them argues that men with such power would not let their own die. Then the demand: heal us. Arc end.

### Arc II. Malhado (chapters 31 to 60)

31. **Physicians.** Blow on the sick, make the sign of the cross, say a Pater Noster and an Ave Maria. Food withheld until they comply. They comply. The sick say they are well.
32. **Left behind, spring 1529.** Cabeza de Vaca near death. Dorantes, Castillo, and about twelve go south along the coast. Left: Cabeza de Vaca, Lope de Oviedo, Alaniz. Alaniz dies.
33. **Roots.** Digging roots under water among the reeds until his fingers bleed. Mosquitoes. The three months of oysters. Servant to the Capoques.
34. **Their ways.** They love their children more than any people he has known. A dead child is mourned a year. The young are buried; old healers are burned. Record it the way he does, plainly.
35. **The trader.** To the mainland and the Charrucos. He becomes a merchant: seashells, conch cores for cutting, sea beans inland; hides, red ochre, flint, arrow cane, deer-hair tassels back. Free to travel. Known everywhere.
36. **The trading years.** Forty leagues inland and back. Alone in a land with no map. Level 5 begins, the first verbs: camino, como, duermo.
37. **Coming back for Lope.** Every year he returns to the island for Lope de Oviedo. Every year Lope refuses to go.
38. **The fourth year.** Lope agrees. They cross four rivers on the coast with the Deaguanes. At a bay the Quevenes tell them three men like them are alive inland among the Mariames and Yguases, and that the others are dead.
39. **Lope turns back.** The Quevenes strike them and threaten worse. Lope goes back with the Deaguanes women. Cabeza de Vaca goes on alone.
40. **Reunion.** The Mariames. Dorantes, Castillo, Estebanico alive. Two days of joy and telling. The others: Esquivel killed by the Mariames over a dream. Figueroa's story.
41. **What Figueroa said.** Narváez stayed aboard his barge one night with a helmsman and a page. A north wind carried it out. Never seen again. The rest wintered in the marsh and died one by one, and the living ate the dead.
42. **The Mariames.** He records that they kill their daughters at birth so enemies cannot marry them, and gives their reasons. Dogs eaten in hunger. The tuna season the only good time of year. Plainly. No lecture in either direction.
43. **Servitude.** He carries wood and water and is beaten. Dorantes and Estebanico with the Mariames, Castillo with the Yguases. Plan: escape at the prickly pear grounds where all the peoples gather.
44. **First tuna season, 1533.** The escape fails. A quarrel over a woman splits the camps and the Spaniards are separated again. Another year.
45. **The year of waiting.** Spiders, ant eggs, worms, lizards, snakes, earth, wood, deer dung. He lists it all. Level 6 begins: third person, a line of dialogue in Spanish.
46. **Second tuna season, September 1534.** New moon. They slip away to the Avavares. First night free in six years.
47. **Castillo heals.** Men with headaches. The sign of the cross. The whole camp brings tunas and venison. They cannot eat it all.
48. **The dead man.** Eyes rolled back, no pulse, a mat over him. Cabeza de Vaca removes the mat, prays, breathes on him. That night the man sits up and eats. He writes only that God was served. Do not explain it.
49. **Winter with the Avavares.** Eight months. Naked. They shed their skin twice a year like snakes. Cold so deep the fire is the only fact.
50. **Mala Cosa.** The Avavares tell of a small bearded man who came fifteen or sixteen years ago, entered houses, cut people open, took out entrails, and healed the cuts. The Spaniards laugh. The Avavares show the scars. Cabeza de Vaca tells them it is evil and will not return if they believe in God. Leave it there.
51. **Lost.** Five days alone gathering tunas fruit. North wind, naked. A burning tree. He digs a pit, keeps four fires all night, and burns his hair.
52. **Hunger road.** Maliacones, Arbadaos. They trade nets and a hide for two dogs to eat.
53. **Hides.** He scrapes deer hides for others and eats the scrapings. Thorns. The body as ledger.
54. **The custom.** A village that receives the healers may plunder the next. The escort hands them on. He finds this hard and cannot stop it. Level 7 begins: full sentences in the present.
55. **Inland.** They turn away from the coast and the coastal peoples toward the mountains, following word of maize.
56. **Houses.** The big river at La Junta de los Ríos. The first permanent houses since Florida. Beans, squash. Bison hides.
57. **Las vacas.** The bison: like our cows, he writes, with small horns and long wool. He has been carrying that word in his own name for a year of chapters. Now it is an animal he can see.
58. **Seventeen days.** Across the plains on deer fat. Then the mountains.
59. **Sunset.** Castillo wants the coast. Cabeza de Vaca argues for the west, toward the sunset, where the maize is. They follow the maize.
60. **The bell.** In the mountains they are given a copper bell with a face on it, from the north, where there is much of it. The first metal since the wreck. Arc end.

### Arc III. Hijos del sol (chapters 61 to 90)

61. **Corazones.** The valley in Sonora where they are given six hundred deer hearts. Cotton blankets, turquoise, coral. Level 8 begins: the preterite arrives with the walk.
62. **Estebanico ahead.** He goes first into every village, learns the signs, does the talking. The four are one man's voice and three men's blessing. Write it from his side for once. (His interior is invented; his role is not.)
63. **The poison tree.** Arrows tipped with the milk of a tree that kills. Sonora.
64. **Every person.** No one eats until all have been blessed. Children brought in their hundreds. Exhaustion as a daily fact.
65. **The arrowhead.** A man with an arrow point lodged near his heart. Cabeza de Vaca cuts it out with a knife, stitches with a bone needle, dresses it with deer hide. Two days later the man is well. The greatest fame of all.
66. **Empty country.** Beautiful land, deserted. Houses burned. People hiding in the mountains, planting nothing. Signs.
67. **The buckle.** A man wears a sword-belt buckle around his neck with a horseshoe nail sewn to it. Asked, he says bearded men came from the sky with horses, lances, and swords, and killed two of them. Arc hinge.
68. **What the Christians do.** The land is empty because Spaniards raid it for slaves. The people starve rather than plant. Cabeza de Vaca's anger, recorded plainly.
69. **Tracks.** Horse tracks. Stakes where horses were tied. Cabeza de Vaca and Estebanico go ahead with eleven Indians.
70. **Four horsemen.** They stare at a naked man with Indians and do not speak. He asks to be taken to their captain. Diego de Alcaraz.
71. **Alcaraz.** Culiacán is thirty leagues. He complains that he cannot find Indians to take. Cabeza de Vaca asks him for a certificate of the date and place of their arrival. The treasurer, still. Level 9 begins: paragraphs in Spanish.
72. **Six hundred.** Dorantes and Castillo arrive with six hundred people. Alcaraz wants them as slaves. The four refuse.
73. **Not the same people.** The Indians will not believe the four are Spaniards like these. These men lie. We came from the sunrise, they from the sunset. We heal, they kill. We came naked and gave everything, they came clothed and take. Close to the source's words.
74. **Sent away.** Separated from the Indians. Marched under guard through the wilderness by a route Cabeza de Vaca believes was meant to kill them. Culiacán. Melchior Díaz weeps.
75. **Calling them back.** Díaz asks the four to send for the people in the mountains. They come down, are baptized, plant again. A peace, for now.
76. **Clothes.** He cannot bear them for many days and sleeps on the ground.
77. **Mexico City, July 1536.** Compostela, Nuño de Guzmán. Then the capital, the day before the feast of Santiago. Viceroy Mendoza and Cortés receive them. Bullfights.
78. **The Joint Report.** Dorantes, Castillo, and Cabeza de Vaca write down what happened for the Audiencia. The first time the story becomes words.
79. **Estebanico's road.** He stays in New Spain, in the viceroy's service. In 1539 he leads Fray Marcos de Niza's party north toward the cities of Cíbola and is killed at Hawikuh. The record gives him one line. Give him a chapter.
80. **Dorantes and Castillo.** They marry widows in New Spain and stay. The walk ends differently for each man. Level 10 begins: passages of the 1542 text.
81. **The Azores, 1537.** French corsairs chase the ship home. The Portuguese fleet saves it. Lisbon in August.
82. **Writing.** Spain, 1537 to 1540. He writes the *Relación* for the King. Having no gold to bring, he brings this: what he saw, the roads, the peoples. The treasurer's only treasure is the account.
83. **What he argues.** The people can be brought to God and the Crown only by good treatment, and by nothing else. In his words, modernized.
84. **The passage.** A chapter that is a single passage from the 1542 text, the weeping on the beach, read in Spanish with the English only for what the reader has never met.
85. **Río de la Plata, 1540.** Appointed governor. Sails. Marches overland to Asunción through country no European has crossed.
86. **The fall.** Arrested by the colonists in 1544, shipped home in chains, tried, sentenced, exiled. The Crown later softens it.
87. **Jerez.** An old man in Seville or Jerez, the record is thin. Died around 1559. He never went back.
88. **Malhado revisited.** A return, in memory, to the island: the archers, the pot, the dog, the fires along the path. In Spanish now.
89. **The dead man.** The healing at the Avavares camp again, this time in his words and only his words.
90. **The rope.** The barge, the wind, Narváez's answer. The last chapter is the first one he would have written if he had let himself. Then the shore.

Chapters 84 and 88 to 90 revisit earlier scenes so the reader can feel the progress bar: the same beach, now in Spanish. That is the whole point of the app and the season should end on it.

## Chapter rules specific to this story

- **One sourced beat per chapter, minimum.** Every chapter advances by something that is in the *Relación* or the Joint Report. Texture, dialogue, and interior are ours; the spine is his.
- **Flag inventions.** Anything not in the source gets `(invented)` in the running summary in `CONTENT-GUIDE.md`. Named minor characters not in the record are inventions and should be rare. Do not invent names for Indigenous people; use the names and roles the source gives.
- **Quantities are his.** Where he gives a number (forty-two horses, two pounds of biscuit, six hundred hearts, fifteen alive), use it exactly or not at all. Do not round for rhythm.
- **Do not explain the wonders.** Healings, Mala Cosa, the weeping, the tree burning in the storm. Report and move on.
- **The hard passages stay.** Cannibalism at Xamho, infanticide among the Mariames as he reports it, slavery on the Sinaloa. Brief, plain, no gore, no editorial. The reader is an adult.
- **Estebanico is a person.** Never "the Moor" as a tag. He has judgment and it is often the judgment that saves them.
- **The peoples are people.** Generosity and cruelty both, with reasons. No noble savage, no chorus of extras.
- **The Spanish teaches the survival core.** Prefer a word the reader will use in a kitchen or on a trail over one that only lives in this story.
- **Level 10 passages are modernized, not translated.** Update spelling (fasta to hasta, dellos to de ellos), keep his syntax where a modern reader can follow it, and cite the chapter of the 1542 text in the JSON `hook` or a comment in the guide table.

## Open decisions

- Whether Arc III should be thirty chapters or shorter. The Sonora to Sinaloa stretch is dense in the source; the Spanish years after 1537 are thin. The current outline pads 85 to 87. Decide after Arc I is written and the pacing is known. If the count changes, re-derive the weave ramp in `CONTENT-GUIDE.md` so the last chapter still lands at the top.
- Whether Estebanico's chapter (79) belongs in Arc III or as a standalone epilogue outside the season.
- Whether to keep the frame of the narrator writing in Spain (82) or let the present tense run to the end.
